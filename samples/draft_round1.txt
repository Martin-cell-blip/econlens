The article discusses a proposed 20% tax on sugary drinks. Sugary drinks are a
demerit good because they are overconsumed and create a negative externality of
consumption: the marginal social benefit of consumption lies below the marginal
private benefit, since obesity-related treatment costs of $48 million fall
partly on taxpayers rather than on drinkers themselves.

As Figure 1 shows, the market equilibrium quantity is greater than the socially
optimal quantity, creating a welfare loss. An indirect tax of 20% shifts the
supply curve upward, raising price and reducing quantity demanded toward the
social optimum. Because demand from teenagers is estimated to fall by up to 15%,
demand appears relatively elastic for this group, so the tax may be effective at
reducing consumption where the health risk is greatest.

This is an example of government intervention to correct market failure. The
$12 million in revenue earmarked for school sports programmes could further
increase economic well-being.

However, the tax is likely regressive: low-income households spend a larger
share of income on these drinks, so they bear a larger burden. In the short run,
cross-border shopping in the neighbouring county could blunt the effect, as the
article notes, and 300 retail jobs may be at risk. In the long run, producers
may reformulate drinks with less sugar, which would reduce the externality at
its source. On balance, the tax is justified because the fall in consumption of
22% seen in the nearby state suggests the intervention works, but the government
should consider using part of the revenue to offset the burden on low-income
households.
